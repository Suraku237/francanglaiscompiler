\section*{Abstract and Evidence Statement}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract and Evidence Statement}
Camfranglais is a compiler-construction teaching application for examining
informal expressions without replacing their wording with standardized French
or English. The implementation combines a regular-expression tokenizer,
priority-ordered lexical rules, explicit reference vocabulary, conservative
language-transition clues and a table-driven LL(1) parser. It deliberately
separates vocabulary recognition from membership in a selected category grammar.

This report evaluates twelve exact transcriptions supplied to the project and
preserved in Collection. One controlled pass produces \CorpusTokens{} tokens
and \CorpusForms{} case-folded forms. The derived grammar has ten original
nonterminals and 37 alternatives. Actual elimination of direct left recursion
and left factoring produce twelve nonterminals, \GrammarProductions{}
alternatives and \GrammarCells{} populated predictive-table cells with no
conflict. Ten statements parse completely. Two reject after five consumed
tokens because their sixth tokens, \code{n'ais} and \code{alli}, are unresolved.
Neither word is silently corrected.

The application is publicly accessible without accounts. Visitors may analyze
and retain immutable test snapshots; Collection, grammar and recordings remain
read-only. Browser screenshots in this report were captured from the deployed
public application on 27 September 2026 without submitting additional tests.
Historical aggregate statistics in those screenshots differ from this report's
fixed twelve-statement evaluation.

\section{Context, Problem and Objectives}
\subsection*{The assignment and the problem}
CS4110 SET A asks a group of three to examine 10--15 real-life expressions
heard in Yaound\'e, preserving slang, code-mixing, incomplete speech and
nonstandard forms. The required compiler work includes token categories and
regular expressions, frequency and variation analysis, a grammar constructed
from the expressions, grammar transformations, FIRST/FOLLOW, a parsing table
and accepted/rejected test cases. The chosen parsing route here is LL(1);
an LR automaton is not additionally required for that route.

The educational challenge is that source-code languages usually have an
explicitly agreed syntax, whereas everyday speech is contextual and variable.
A token such as \code{school} may occur within a French-framed expression.
Different spellings may suggest the same intention without proving equivalence.
An apparent mistake may be part of the original transcription rather than
permission for the software to rewrite it. A useful teaching tool must expose
its assumptions instead of masking them behind a successful-looking result.

Ebongue's study distinguishes speaker-written Camfranglais from researcher
transcriptions and discusses variation in spelling, syntax and morphosyntax
\cite{ebongue}. That distinction motivates caution here: stored text and
computed categories are observable, but a small unverified textual sample
cannot establish how a community speaks or how a particular speaker intended
an expression.

\subsection*{Operational objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Preserve all twelve supplied strings and label every token in source order.
\item Implement deterministic recognition rules and expose unknowns, ambiguous
labels, language clues and corpus frequencies.
\item Derive reusable category productions from observed structures rather
than hard-code a whitelist of twelve complete sentences.
\item Perform actual grammar transformations and compute every set/table cell.
\item Demonstrate predictive parsing, successful consumption and precise
failure positions, including examples outside the supplied corpus.
\item Present an accessible public workspace with durable, immutable test
evidence and no unnecessary account workflow.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Non-objectives}
This is not a general Camfranglais translator, speech recognizer, generative
assistant, spelling corrector or semantic parser. It does not infer speaker
identity, indigenous-language origin, consent or authenticity. The present
report documents completed compiler behavior while identifying the unresolved
fieldwork requirement rather than claiming it has been satisfied automatically.

\section{Custom Lexical Specification}
\subsection*{Segmentation by regular expression}
The tokenizer recognizes Unicode word-like runs, internal apostrophes/hyphens,
decimal numbers, selected punctuation and finally any remaining non-whitespace
character. The final alternative is important: unsupported input is preserved
for classification rather than silently discarded.

The following factored notation describes the actual segmentation pattern.
\code{M} denotes the combining-mark ranges U+0300--036F, U+1AB0--1AFF,
U+1DC0--1DFF, U+20D0--20FF and U+FE20--FE2F. Substituting those ranges and
\code{L} into the definitions yields the pattern in the source; spaces around
the displayed alternatives are notation, not characters to match.
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
L     = [^\W\d_]+(?:[M]+[^\W\d_]*)*
WORD  = L(?:['\u2018\u2019\u02bc-]L)*
TOKEN = WORD | \d+(?:[.,]\d+)? | [.,!?;:"()] | \S
\end{verbatim}}

Internal apostrophes therefore keep \code{J'ai}, \code{n'est} and
\code{n'ais} as single tokens. The twelve supplied statements have no explicit
punctuation tokens, but punctuation behavior is exercised by separate controls.
Whitespace separates matches; the original full input, including its whitespace,
is preserved independently of the token list.

\subsection*{Regex classification and fallback}
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
NUMBER       ^\d+(?:[.,]\d+)?$
PUNCTUATION  ^[.,!?;:"()]+$
ENGLISH_VERB_LIKE  suffix "ing" or "ed"
FRENCH_VERB_LIKE   suffix "er", "ir" or "re", length > 3
\end{verbatim}}

Only the first two rules are structural full matches applied before lookup.
The suffix checks operate on normalized words \emph{after} explicit lexical
sources have failed. A \code{\_LIKE} label is a morphological guess, not a
confirmed part of speech. A word ending in an apparent verbal suffix can
have a different function in context.

\subsection*{Deterministic pipeline}
Tokenize the raw input; classify each token by the first applicable rule;
retain each raw token and its chosen category; add phrase and language clues
without altering the token stream; compute statistics; pass categories to the
parser. There is no random model, translation provider or automatic spelling
correction. The implementation is in
\nolinkurl{compiler/lexer/tokenizer.py} and
\nolinkurl{compiler/lexer/lexicon.py} \cite{source}.

\section{Lexicon, Category Policy and Notation}
\subsection*{Precedence is part of the result}
After NUMBER/PUNCTUATION, a compatible approved, unambiguous Word annotation may
override bundled lookup. Conflicting reviewed labels are ignored rather than
arbitrarily resolved. The snapshot's approved \code{choa} annotation does not
occur in these twelve sentences.

Explicit lookup then proceeds through SLANG, PIDGIN\_MARKER, NOUN, VERB,
French function words and English function words. The supplied CSV fills
remaining gaps; one category is retained, multiple candidates yield
AMBIGUOUS. Explicit French forms supplement gaps, then morphological guesses
and finally UNKNOWN apply. Existing explicit labels take precedence over new
reference labels. This explains why a dictionary origin or apparent contextual
meaning does not automatically determine the emitted category.

The dictionary contains \DictionaryCount{} display entries from
\ReferenceRows{} supplied rows plus 60 retained distinct legacy senses.
Dictionary entries are source-labelled references, not collected utterances.
Alias normalization can support lookup without changing displayed spelling.
Multiword references annotate phrases separately; they do not assign a whole
phrase's category to each component word.

Colloquial words need not receive the literal SLANG label. For example,
\code{kass} is ADJ and \code{nang} is V under the current lookup. The category
system is a software convention, not an exhaustive sociolinguistic taxonomy.

\section{Code-Mixing, Phrases and Linguistic Complexity}
Language clues are separate from part of speech. Word lists and explicit
reference origins provide candidate sets for FR, EN and PID. Shared forms can
have several candidates. The algorithm narrows compatible runs and emits the
last anchor and new token when evidence becomes incompatible. UNKNOWN carries
no language evidence; sentence-ending punctuation resets the run.

{\small\input{report-data/language-spans}}

There are \CorpusSwitches{} emitted clues, not 26 proven language switches.
An ellipsis denotes endpoints and can span intervening tokens; it is neither
a quoted contiguous phrase nor a claim that every intervening word belongs to
one language. No verb-phrase or slang-phrase regex matches these twelve inputs,
although the raw expressions still contain colloquial words and constructions.
The tool must report zero matches rather than fabricate phrase evidence.

French framing around words such as \code{school}, \code{light} and
\code{price} illustrates the interaction between grammar, borrowing and
code-mixing. A fixed word origin cannot determine whether a speaker perceives a
form as a switch or an established borrowing. Ellipsis, juxtaposition, local
idioms and contextual meanings also exceed the information represented by a
category sequence.

Written variation must be distinguished from pronunciation and from a
transcriber's choices \cite{ebongue}. The available material cannot support
claims about speakers' ages, education, neighborhoods, accent or intentions.
S04 and S11 emit no transition clue; that absence is limited evidence, not a
certificate that the originals are monolingual.

\section{Constructing a Corpus-Derived Grammar}
The chosen CFG models recurring lexer-category structures instead of listing
twelve raw sentences. The original has ten nonterminals and 37 alternatives.
For compact printing, Ut, Cl, St, Bt, Pt, Lp, Mn, At, Ct and Nm abbreviate
Utterance, Clause, SubjectTail, BareSubjectTail, PredicateTail, LinkedPhrase,
ModifiedNominal, AdverbTail, ComplementTail and Nominal respectively.

{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}\input{report-data/grammar-original}}

\subsection*{Evidence-to-rule rationale}
Cl permits a function-word subject, a nominal opening or a verb-initial
clause. St covers \code{je suis}, \code{mon combi came} and the observed
AMB--PID pattern in \code{ca me chop}. Pt permits auxiliary/serial verbs
(\code{j'ai mban}, \code{go nang}), observed V--EF--V links, and nominal,
adjectival, adverbial or ambiguous complements.

Lp and Mn represent introduced groups such as \code{au piol},
\code{depuis bayar} and \code{depuis le shap}. Ct allows juxtaposition, as in
\code{je suis kass je go nang} and \code{go au piol shoua mes kako}.
Nm represents noun clusters; its natural left recursion is intentionally
processed by the real transformation routine.

Ut permits one optional punctuation token, although the twelve inputs lack
one. This narrowly stated extension is tested separately. UNKNOWN is excluded,
and AMB is allowed only in explicit positions. A new empty installation may
use an illustrative starter grammar; that starter is not the saved grammar
evaluated in this report.

\section{Actual Grammar Transformations}
\subsection*{Step 1: eliminate direct left recursion}
The implementation detects the original left-recursive nonterminal Nm:
\[
  \mathrm{Nm}\rightarrow\mathrm{Nm}\ \mathrm{N}\mid\mathrm{N}.
\]
It introduces \code{Nominal\_LR1}, abbreviated Nr, and rewrites:
\[
  \mathrm{Nm}\rightarrow\mathrm{N}\ \mathrm{Nr},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Nr}\rightarrow\mathrm{N}\ \mathrm{Nr}\mid\epsilon .
\]
Both forms generate one or more noun-category tokens. The original extends
a noun chain by left recursion; the transformed form consumes the first noun
and extends it through a right-recursive suffix. No raw noun is deleted and
the language of this subgrammar is unchanged.

\subsection*{Step 2: left-factor the start rule}
The original start rule has a shared prefix:
\[
  \mathrm{Ut}\rightarrow\mathrm{Cl}\mid\mathrm{Cl}\ \mathrm{PUNC}.
\]
The routine introduces \code{Utterance\_LF1}, abbreviated Uf:
\[
  \mathrm{Ut}\rightarrow\mathrm{Cl}\ \mathrm{Uf},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Uf}\rightarrow\epsilon\mid\mathrm{PUNC}.
\]
The alternative choice is postponed until the clause has been processed.
End-of-input selects epsilon; PUNC selects the optional punctuation. The
empty alternative is a suffix, not permission for an empty whole utterance.

\subsection*{What the implementation actually logged}
The two operation identifiers are
\nolinkurl{eliminate_direct_left_recursion} and \nolinkurl{left_factor}.
Each record contains the complete grammar before and after the operation.
No ordered indirect-substitution step was needed for this particular rule
ordering. The generic implementation supports bounded ordered substitution
and separately checks for residual nullable-prefix recursion.

The final result has twelve nonterminals, \GrammarProductions{} alternatives,
no residual left-recursive nonterminal and no LL(1) conflict. Transformation
limits reject degenerate cycles or unsafe expansion instead of claiming
success. The regression suite also compares original and transformed
languages with an independent bounded derivation oracle up to four terminals;
that is useful finite evidence, not a proof for every arbitrary CFG.

It is not enough to say that recursion and common prefixes were ``handled''.
The two before/after rewrites above are the actual transformations computed
for this saved corpus grammar.}

\section{FIRST and FOLLOW Sets}
FIRST contains possible initial terminals of a derivation and epsilon when
the nonterminal is nullable. FOLLOW contains terminals, or the end marker
\code{\$}, that can immediately follow a nonterminal in a derivation from the
start symbol. Epsilon does not belong in FOLLOW.

{\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}\input{report-data/first-follow}}

\subsection*{Fixed-point computation}
For each production, FIRST accumulates the first symbol's terminal evidence,
continuing through nullable prefixes and adding epsilon only when the entire
right-hand side is nullable. The implementation repeats these updates until
no set grows. FOLLOW starts by adding \code{\$} to Ut. For each nonterminal
within a right-hand side, FIRST of the suffix contributes non-epsilon
terminals; a nullable or absent suffix also contributes FOLLOW of the left
side. Those updates likewise continue to a fixed point.

\subsection*{Sanity checks on this grammar}
FIRST(Ut) is exactly \{FF, N, V\}; hence empty input cannot begin a clause.
FOLLOW(Ut) contains only end-of-input. Uf is nullable and permits punctuation,
which explains PUNC in FOLLOW(Cl). Nr can begin another N or become empty,
but its FOLLOW set is \{\$, FF, PUNC, V\}, not UNKNOWN.

These sets are computed from the transformed productions, not handwritten
independently for the report. Reporting the full sets allows a reader to verify
the table's epsilon entries and to explain why both unresolved sixth tokens
lead to rejection.

\section{Complete LL(1) Predictive Table}
For \(A\rightarrow\alpha\), each terminal in
\(\mathrm{FIRST}(\alpha)\setminus\{\epsilon\}\) receives that production.
If \(\alpha\) is nullable, its production is also placed under every terminal
in FOLLOW(A). Multiple distinct productions in one cell are conflicts;
the implementation retains the conflict and does not choose an arbitrary
winner.

The complete table is printed below. Columns are the nine grammar terminals
plus end-of-input. The 47 populated cells refer to P01--P39 in the catalogue.
``--'' means no production and causes rejection for that nonterminal/lookahead.

{\scriptsize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
\input{report-data/ll1-table}}

\subsection*{Worked entries}
\begin{itemize}
\item \(M[\mathrm{Ut},\mathrm{FF}]=\mathrm{P01}\), since Cl can begin with FF
and Uf follows it. The same production is selected under N and V.
\item \(M[\mathrm{Pt},\mathrm{ADJ}]=\mathrm{P12}\), selecting
\(\mathrm{Pt}\rightarrow\mathrm{ADJ}\ \mathrm{Ct}\).
\item \(M[\mathrm{Pt},\$]=\mathrm{P18}\), since the Pt alternative is empty
and end-of-input is in FOLLOW(Pt).
\item \(M[\mathrm{Nr},\mathrm{N}]=\mathrm{P36}\), whereas FF, V, PUNC and
\code{\$} select its empty alternative P37.
\item Uf selects P38 at \code{\$} and P39 at PUNC. No row has a defined
UNKNOWN entry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{LL(1) conclusion and limits}
No cell has competing productions, and the transformed grammar has no residual
left recursion. Predictive parsing is therefore enabled for this grammar.
This does not imply that every Camfranglais expression is derivable: a valid
everyday statement can still have a category sequence outside the modeled
subset. It also does not imply that a derived category sequence has a sensible
meaning. Formal determinism and linguistic coverage are different properties.

\section{Predictive Parser Implementation}
The parser maintains a stack, a category sequence, a current input position
and a trace. The stack is printed with its top at the right. Trace snapshots
are taken \emph{before} the recorded action; the final accept row still displays
the end marker. A production is pushed in reverse order so its leftmost symbol
is processed next.

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
stack = ["$", start_symbol]
position = 0
while stack is not empty:
    top = stack[-1]
    lookahead = categories[position] or end marker
    if top is "$":
        accept only if lookahead is also "$"
    elif top is a terminal:
        reject if top != lookahead
        record the match; pop; advance position
    else:
        reject if table[top, lookahead] is absent
        record the chosen production
        pop top; push its RHS in reverse order
\end{verbatim}}

The displayed pseudocode summarizes the control flow; the implementation also
validates token shapes/categories, refuses conflicting grammars and checks
resource limits. It detects repeated stack/input configurations, excessive
trace length and oversized stack expansion. UNKNOWN is a real lexer category
that can be represented in input, but it is not a wildcard and has no production
in this saved grammar.

\toprule\textbf{Parser bound} & \textbf{Value}\\\midrule
Input tokens & 256\\
Characters in an individual token & 1,000\\
Recorded parse steps & 2,000\\
Stack symbols & 1,024\\

\end{tabularx}

The output includes \code{accepted}, an explicit error or null, the trace and
the consumed count. A CFG rejection is a valid computed test result and can be
saved; it is not a network error. Conversely, a storage failure must not look
like a successful saved test. This separation helps students distinguish
lexical uncertainty, syntactic mismatch and operational failure.

\subsection*{Explanation of the derivation}
P02 chooses the FF-led clause. After matching \code{je}, P05 provides a verb
and predicate tail. P12 selects an adjective complement for \code{kass}.
P32 consumes the next FF and P19 allows a following verb, modeling the
juxtaposition rather than insisting on punctuation or a conjunction.

P10 permits the second verb in \code{go nang}. Once all six input categories
have been consumed, P18 removes the empty predicate tail and P38 removes the
optional punctuation suffix. The final row sees only the two end markers and
accepts. The consumed count is six and the error field is null.

\subsection*{What acceptance does and does not mean}
This is evidence that the observed category pattern belongs to the transformed
grammar. It does not validate spelling, supply the expression's intended
meaning, or identify the language of each token. Vocabulary approval also
passes because no UNKNOWN occurs, but that is a separate calculation.
The contrast becomes visible in the constructed known-word controls, where
vocabulary passes and CFG parsing rejects.

\section{Conclusion and Group Review}
The project demonstrates an inspectable compiler pipeline rather than a
black-box language assistant. It preserves all twelve supplied transcriptions,
classifies all 81 token occurrences, computes frequency and language clues,
transforms a genuinely corpus-derived grammar and exposes the complete
conflict-free LL(1) table. Successful and unsuccessful traces show how category
decisions propagate into parsing instead of hiding failures.

The two rejected cases are informative. They identify precise unresolved
forms at token six rather than treating every input as acceptable. The known-word
controls also reject invalid category orders, proving that parsing is more
than a dictionary-membership check. Conversely, acceptance of nonstandard
wording illustrates why the application must keep grammar fit separate from
linguistic correctness and supplied meaning.

The final public interface removes irrelevant account management while
retaining server-side boundaries: anyone can analyze and save an immutable
test, but reference data and grammar cannot be changed through the public app.
The deployed browser was observed functioning in this mode. The separate
engineering documents describe the actual classes and interactions rather
than the older authenticated product.

\subsection*{Priorities after this report}
The highest-priority academic action is provenance confirmation, not another
feature: establish the original fieldwork context or retain the explicit
limitation. Then review the two unknowns and disputed lexical labels using
human evidence, evaluate a genuinely independent corpus, address the stale
category regression, and rehearse the required ten-minute demonstration.
Any corrected or extended analysis should be saved as new evidence, not
silently substituted into original immutable tests.
